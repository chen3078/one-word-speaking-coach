# Question Modules

Use these as selection menus, not mandatory checklists. For a concrete topic, choose about 8–12 questions that create a coherent path from easy observation to useful speech. Use fewer when additional questions would be artificial, obscure, or repetitive.

## Shared progression

Prefer this order where it fits:

1. identity and category;
2. observable appearance, size, shape, color, sound, taste, smell, or texture;
3. parts, contents, ingredients, material, or key features;
4. varieties, states, function, or behavior;
5. time, season, or occasion;
6. multiple common places, sources, or access routes;
7. methods of using, eating, wearing, operating, or preparing it;
8. origin, growth, production, or operating process;
9. personal connection;
10. short action, choice, or comparison.

## Food and drink

Useful dimensions: category, appearance, parts or ingredients, taste and texture, season or occasion, place of purchase, preparation, eating method, personal preference, origin or growth.

Possible questions:

- What is it?
- What does it look like?
- What color is it?
- What does it have inside?
- What does it taste like?
- Can it be hot, cold, fresh, cooked, or prepared in different ways?
- When do people usually eat or drink it?
- Where can we buy it?
- What are some other places where we can get it?
- How do we prepare or eat it?
- How does it grow or how is it made?
- Do you like it? Why or why not?

Do not ask about growth when the food is manufactured and the manufacturing process is not useful at the learner's level. Use preparation instead.

When asking about purchase or access, expand beyond the learner's personal store or brand when other common routes are useful. For example: home, a café, a restaurant, a supermarket, a convenience store, a market, or an online shop. Teach only options that fit the topic.

## Animals

Useful dimensions: category, appearance, body parts, habitat, food, movement, abilities, behavior, life cycle, human connection.

Possible questions:

- What kind of animal is it?
- What does it look like?
- What body parts does it have?
- Where does it live?
- What does it eat?
- How does it move?
- What can it do?
- How does it communicate?
- Is it wild or domestic?
- Have you ever seen one?

Avoid obscure anatomy and unsafe claims about interacting with wild animals.

## Everyday objects and clothing

Useful dimensions: identity, appearance, material, parts, location, ownership, function, method, occasion, care.

Possible questions:

- What is it?
- What does it look like?
- What is it made of?
- What parts does it have?
- What do we use it for?
- When do we use or wear it?
- Where do we usually keep it?
- How do we use it?
- Do you have one?
- Which kind do you prefer?

## Transport

Useful dimensions: category, appearance and parts, power source, passengers, place, purpose, method, speed or comparison, personal experience.

Possible questions:

- What kind of transport is it?
- What parts does it have?
- How does it move?
- Where can it go?
- What do people use it for?
- How many people can use it?
- How do we use it safely?
- Is it faster or slower than ...?
- How often do you use it?

## Places

Useful dimensions: type, location, appearance, people, objects, activities, time, access, rules, personal experience.

Possible questions:

- What kind of place is it?
- What can we see there?
- Who works or spends time there?
- What can people do there?
- When do people usually go there?
- How do you get there?
- What should or shouldn't people do there?
- How often do you go there?
- What do you like about it?

## People and occupations

Useful dimensions: role, workplace, tools, duties, schedule, skills, clothing, contribution, personal connection.

Possible questions:

- What does this person do?
- Where do they work?
- What tools do they use?
- What do they usually wear?
- What skills do they need?
- How do they help people?
- When do they work?
- Do you know someone who does this job?
- Would you like to do this job? Why?

Avoid stereotyping gender, status, personality, or ability.

## Nature and weather

Useful dimensions: identity, appearance, location, time or season, cause, change, effects, safety, feelings.

Possible questions:

- What does it look or feel like?
- Where can we see it?
- When does it usually happen?
- What happens before or after it?
- How does it affect people, animals, or plants?
- What do people usually do when it happens?
- How can people stay safe?
- Do you like this kind of weather or natural scene?

Keep causal explanations simple and accurate.

## Actions and activities

Useful dimensions: meaning, actor, place, time, equipment, sequence, ability, frequency, safety, preference.

Possible questions:

- What does this action mean?
- Who can do it?
- Where can people do it?
- What do we need to do it?
- How do we do it?
- When do you usually do it?
- Can you do it?
- How often do you do it?
- Why do people do it?
- Do you enjoy it?

Use a short first–then–finally sequence only when the action has meaningful steps.

## Feelings and abstract beginner topics

Useful dimensions: simple meaning, situation, physical or behavioral signs, cause, response, opposite, example, personal connection.

Possible questions:

- What does it mean?
- When do people feel or use it?
- What can make someone feel this way?
- What might a person say or do?
- What is a simple example?
- What is the opposite?
- What can help in this situation?
- When did you last feel this way?

Do not force appearance, parts, purchase location, or other concrete-object questions onto an abstract topic.

## Personalization boundaries

Prefer low-pressure daily questions. Let the learner decline or choose another example. Do not ask for sensitive health, financial, legal, sexual, religious, political, workplace-confidential, or identifying information merely to personalize practice.
